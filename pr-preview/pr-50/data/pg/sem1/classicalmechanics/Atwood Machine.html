The Atwood Machine: Watching a Lagrangian Turn Into Motion

One string, one pulley, two masses, and a single generalized coordinate that describes the whole system. This module builds the Lagrangian from first principles, then hands you the controls to test what it predicts.

01

Concept

The Atwood machine is two masses, m1 and m2, joined by a light inextensible string that runs over a pulley. It looks trivial, but it is one of the cleanest systems for showing why the Lagrangian formalism is worth learning: instead of resolving forces on two free bodies and eliminating the string tension by hand, we track a single generalized coordinate and let the variational structure of mechanics do the rest.

The physical system

  • Mass 1 (m1) hangs on one side of the pulley.
  • Mass 2 (m2) hangs on the other side.
  • A string of fixed length connects them over a pulley of radius R.
  • The pulley may be idealised as massless, or given an effective mass Meff representing its rotational inertia.
  • Gravity g acts downward; the string only pulls, never pushes.

Modelling assumptions

  • The string is inextensible — it does not stretch.
  • The string is massless — it stores no kinetic energy of its own.
  • The string does not slip on the pulley (rolling constraint).
  • The pulley axle is frictionless, unless the friction slider is switched on for an exploration.
  • Motion is one-dimensional: both masses move only vertically.

Coordinate system and the generalized coordinate

Because the string is inextensible, the two masses are not free to move independently: if m1 descends by some distance, m2 must rise by exactly the same distance. Two Cartesian positions are tied together by one constraint equation, so the configuration space of the system is really one-dimensional — it has a single degree of freedom.

Following the standard convention of analytical mechanics, we describe that one degree of freedom with a generalized coordinate q, taken here as the downward displacement of m1 from its starting position. The string constraint fixes the displacement of m2 to be q upward. The generalized velocity is q̇ = dq/dt, and the generalized acceleration is q̈ = dq̇/dt.

Building the Lagrangian

We first state the Euler–Lagrange equation in its general form, for an arbitrary generalized coordinate q, and then specialise it to the Atwood machine.

The general formalism

For a holonomic system with one degree of freedom described by generalized coordinate q and generalized velocity q̇, the Lagrangian is the scalar function

Definition
L(q, q̇)  =  T(q, q̇) − V(q)

and the equation of motion follows from the Euler–Lagrange equation

Euler–Lagrange equation
ddt ∂L∂q̇  −  ∂L∂q  =  0

This one line is the entire content of Lagrangian mechanics for a one-degree-of-freedom system: write down T and V in terms of whatever coordinate actually varies, form L, and differentiate. No free-body diagram and no constraint force ever needs to be drawn.

Step 1 — Kinetic energy T(q, q̇)

Both masses move at speed q̇ (equal magnitude, opposite direction, by the string constraint). If the pulley has effective inertia mass Meff = I / R², it also carries kinetic energy because it must spin as the string moves over it.

Kinetic energy
T  =  12 m1q̇²  +  12 m2q̇²  +  12 Meffq̇²  =  12 (m1 + m2 + Meff) q̇²

Step 2 — Potential energy V(q)

Take the starting position as the reference height. As m1 falls a distance q, its potential energy drops; as m2 rises by the same distance, its potential energy increases.

Potential energy
V  =  −m1gq  +  m2gq  =  (m2 − m1) gq

Step 3 — The Lagrangian

L = T − V
L(q, q̇)  =  12 (m1 + m2 + Meff) q̇²  −  (m2 − m1) gq

Step 4 — Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation

Differentiate L term by term.

Generalized momentum term
∂L∂q̇  =  (m1 + m2 + Meff) q̇   ⇒   ddt ∂L∂q̇  =  (m1 + m2 + Meff) q̈
Generalized force term
∂L∂q  =  −(m2 − m1)g  =  (m1 − m2)g

Substituting into the Euler–Lagrange equation gives the equation of motion:

Equation of motion
(m1 + m2 + Meff) q̈  −  (m1 − m2)g  =  0
Solving for the generalized acceleration
q̈  =  (m1 − m2)gm1 + m2 + Meff

For an ideal massless pulley, Meff = 0, and this reduces to the familiar result q̈ = (m1 − m2)g / (m1 + m2). In the simulator and readouts below, this quantity is also written as a, the linear acceleration of either mass along the string.

Recovering the tension

The Lagrangian, written purely in terms of q, has already eliminated the string tension — it is a workless constraint force and drops out of the reduced equation of motion. To recover it, apply Newton's second law to one mass alone, using the value of q̈ found above.

Mass 1 (moving down)
m1g − T1  =  m1q̈   ⇒   T1 = m1(g − q̈)
Mass 2 (moving up)
T2 − m2g  =  m2q̈   ⇒   T2 = m2(g + q̈)

For a massless, frictionless pulley, T1 = T2. With a heavy pulley (Meff > 0), the two differ slightly — the difference supplies the torque that spins the pulley up.

Conservation of energy

With no friction, gravity is the only force doing work, and it is conservative. Total mechanical energy is therefore constant along the motion:

E  =  T + V  =  12(m1+m2+Meff)q̇²  +  (m2−m1)gq  =  constant

The simulator plots kinetic, potential, and total energy live. Switch on friction in the exploration section and watch the total-energy line slope downward — that is energy leaving the mechanical system as heat at the pulley axle.

03

Exploration

Run each investigation on the simulator above, then fill in your own observation, explanation, and conclusion before checking the reveal. This is where the Lagrangian stops being algebra and starts being a prediction you test.

Investigation 1

Mass difference at constant total mass

Set m1 + m2 = 10 kg throughout. Try the pairs (5,5), (7,3), (9,1), keeping Meff and friction at zero. Record q(t) and the readout acceleration for each.

Reveal expected physics
q̈ = (m1−m2)g/(m1+m2) grows with the mass difference, not the individual masses. (5,5) gives q̈ = 0; (9,1) gives the largest acceleration of the three, close to g, since the light mass barely resists being lifted.
Investigation 2

The balanced case, m1 = m2

Set m1 = m2 = 4 kg and run the simulator. Watch how long it takes to reach the stop condition.

Reveal expected physics
The Euler–Lagrange equation gives q̈ = 0 exactly when m1 = m2: the generalized force (m1−m2)g vanishes. The system stays at rest at any q — a hallmark of zero net generalized force, not a true minimum-energy equilibrium. The simulator will time out rather than hit the pulley or floor.
Investigation 3

Pulley inertia — from idealised to realistic

Fix m1 = 6 kg, m2 = 4 kg. Sweep Meff from 0 to 5 kg and record how the simulated acceleration and the gap between T1 and T2 change.

Reveal expected physics
Increasing M_eff adds to the denominator of q̈ = (m1−m2)g/(m1+m2+M_eff), so acceleration falls. Part of the released gravitational energy now goes into spinning the pulley rather than speeding up the masses. T1 and T2 must differ once M_eff > 0, because their difference supplies the torque needed to spin the pulley.
Investigation 4

Friction and the energy budget

Fix m1 = 6 kg, m2 = 4 kg, Meff = 0. Run once with friction f = 0, then again with f = 4 N. Compare the total-energy plot in both runs.

Reveal expected physics
With f = 0, total mechanical energy stays flat, confirming the system is conservative. With friction, the total-energy line slopes downward — the missing energy has been dissipated as heat at the pulley axle, exactly as a non-conservative generalized force term predicts.
Investigation 5

Changing gravity

Keep m1 = 6 kg, m2 = 4 kg, Meff = 0, f = 0. Run at g = 9.8 (Earth), then g = 1.6 (Moon), then g = 24.8 (Jupiter). Compare acceleration and tension.

Reveal expected physics
Both q̈ and T scale linearly with g, since g appears as an overall multiplicative factor in both formulas. The mass ratio that sets the size of the effect relative to g never changes — only the "clock speed" of the motion does.

Application / Assignment

Problem: the counterweighted service lift

A small maintenance lift is being designed for a two-storey workshop. The lift cabin (with a technician and tools inside) is connected by a cable over a pulley to a fixed counterweight, exactly like an Atwood machine. The building code requires the cabin's acceleration to stay under 1.2 m/s² for passenger comfort and cable safety, and the pulley in the design has a measurable effective mass of 1.5 kg because it is not massless in reality.

  1. Choose a cabin mass m1 between 60 and 100 kg (representing an empty cabin plus one technician) and a counterweight mass m2 between 40 and 90 kg.
  2. By hand, using the boxed formula from the derivation, calculate the theoretical acceleration q̈ and both tensions T1, T2 for g = 9.8 m/s² and M_eff = 1.5 kg.
  3. Enter the same m1, m2, g = 9.8, M_eff = 1.5, and friction = 0 into the simulator above. Run it and record the simulated acceleration from the readout, and take a screenshot or reading of the position, velocity, and energy plots.
  4. Compare your hand-calculated acceleration to the simulated value. Compute the percentage difference and comment on likely sources of any discrepancy (numerical integration step size, reading precision).
  5. Check whether your chosen masses satisfy the 1.2 m/s² comfort constraint. If not, adjust m1 or m2 and repeat steps 2–4 until the design passes.
  6. Plot (by hand or in a spreadsheet, using values you read off the simulator) velocity versus time for your final design, and confirm it matches a straight line through the origin — the signature of constant acceleration.
  7. Real-world connection: explain, in a short paragraph, how this same counterweight principle is used in real elevators and cranes to reduce the motor power needed to lift a load, and why the original 18th-century Atwood machine was built specifically as a laboratory instrument to measure g precisely by slowing free fall down to a measurable rate.

Deliverable: a short write-up (1–2 pages) containing your hand calculation, simulator readouts, the velocity–time plot, the percentage-difference comparison, and your answer to the design constraint and the real-world connection question.

Conclusion

The Atwood machine reduces a two-body constrained problem to a single generalized coordinate, and the Euler–Lagrange equation turns that one coordinate directly into an equation of motion, without ever having to isolate the string tension along the way. The simulator confirms that this derived acceleration, q̈ = (m1−m2)g/(m1+m2+M_eff), matches the numerically integrated motion to within the small error introduced by discretised time-stepping. Adding pulley inertia or friction shows the same method extending gracefully: inertia enters as an added mass term in the denominator, and friction breaks the flat total-energy line predicted for a conservative system. The broader lesson carries beyond this one machine — the Lagrangian method scales to systems where free-body diagrams become unmanageable, because it only ever asks for two scalar quantities, kinetic and potential energy, expressed in whatever coordinate actually varies.
