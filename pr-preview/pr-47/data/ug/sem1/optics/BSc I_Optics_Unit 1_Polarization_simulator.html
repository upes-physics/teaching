2. Background, Concept & Theory

Polarization is a fundamental property of transverse electromagnetic waves describing the geometrical orientation of the electric field vector \(\mathbf{E}(z,t)\) as it propagates along the \(z\)-axis.

An electric field vector can be decomposed into two orthogonal transverse components: \(E_x(z,t) = E_{0x} \cos(kz - \omega t)\) and \(E_y(z,t) = E_{0y} \cos(kz - \omega t + \Delta\phi)\). Depending on the amplitude ratio (\(E_{0y}/E_{0x}\)) and relative phase difference (\(\Delta\phi\)), the light vector traces out distinct polarization states:

  • Linear Polarization: \(\Delta\phi = 0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\). The electric field oscillates in a single plane.
  • Circular Polarization: \(E_{0x} = E_{0y}\) and \(\Delta\phi = \pm 90^\circ\). The vector tip describes a circle.
  • Elliptical Polarization: General case where amplitudes differ or phase \(\Delta\phi \neq 0^\circ, 90^\circ\).
  • Unpolarized Light: Random, rapidly fluctuating polarization orientations across time.
3. Governing Formulas & Equations

Malus's Law

$$I = I_0 \cos^2(\theta_2 - \theta_1)$$
Transmitted intensity through sequential linear polarizers

Brewster's Angle

$$\theta_B = \arctan\left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}\right)$$
Angle for $100\%$ s-polarized reflected light

Waveplate Retardation

$$\Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} |n_e - n_o| d$$
Phase delay introduced between fast and slow optical axes
