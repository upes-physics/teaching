Types of Lattices — 14 Bravais Lattices

Learning Objectives

  • Name the 7 crystal systems and their axial relationships (a, b, c, α, β, γ)
  • Identify all 14 Bravais lattices and the centring types P, I, F, C, R
  • Distinguish a conventional unit cell from a primitive cell and count lattice points
  • Relate lattice geometry to symmetry (characteristic axes, holohedral point group)
  • Connect a lattice to its Miller planes and interplanar spacing dhkl

Theory

Lattice, Basis & Unit Cell
A lattice is an infinite periodic array of points; a basis is the atom(s) attached to each point. Crystal = lattice + basis. The unit cell is the parallelepiped defined by three vectors a, b, c and the angles α, β, γ between them; stacking it fills all space.
The 7 Crystal Systems
Cubic (a=b=c, all 90°) · Tetragonal (a=b≠c) · Orthorhombic (a≠b≠c, all 90°) · Hexagonal (a=b, γ=120°) · Rhombohedral/Trigonal (a=b=c, angles equal but ≠90°) · Monoclinic (only β≠90°) · Triclinic (no constraints). Symmetry decreases down this list.
Centring: P, I, F, C, R
P primitive — points at corners only (1 per cell).
I body-centred — extra point at the centre (2 per cell).
F face-centred — extra point on every face (4 per cell).
C base-centred — extra point on one pair of faces (2 per cell).
R rhombohedral — the trigonal primitive setting (1 per cell).
Only 14 distinct combinations survive; the rest reduce to a smaller cell already in the list.
Conventional vs Primitive Cell
A primitive cell contains exactly one lattice point (volume = V/point). A conventional cell (e.g. the FCC cube) is larger but shows the full symmetry. FCC has 4 lattice points per conventional cell but its primitive cell is a rhombohedron with one point.
Symmetry & Holohedry
Each system has a characteristic symmetry element (cubic: four 3-fold body-diagonal axes; hexagonal: one 6-fold axis; triclinic: only inversion). The full point-group symmetry of the bare lattice is its holohedry — e.g. m3̄m (order 48) for cubic, 1̄ (order 2) for triclinic.
Miller Planes & Reciprocal Lattice
A plane (h k l) cuts the axes at a/h, b/k, c/l. Its spacing follows from the reciprocal lattice:
dhkl = 1 / |h a* + k b* + l c*|
with a* = (b×c)/V, etc. For a cubic crystal this reduces to the familiar
d = a / √(h²+k²+l²).
Common Mistakes
• Treating every cell as a scaled cube — monoclinic and triclinic have genuinely non-orthogonal axes.
• Thinking there are 14 systems (there are 7 systems, 14 lattices).
• Counting a base-centred cubic — it isn't one of the 14 (it reduces to tetragonal P).
• Confusing lattice points per cell with atoms per cell.

Common Mistakes

There are 7 crystal systems but 14 Bravais lattices — the extra ones come from I, F and C centring. A base-centred cubic is not among them: it reduces to a smaller primitive tetragonal cell.

Why Should I Care?

The Bravais lattice fixes which X-ray reflections appear and how bands form. NaCl and Cu are both cubic-F yet behave differently because of their basis; graphite’s hexagonal lattice gives it its layered, lubricating character.
