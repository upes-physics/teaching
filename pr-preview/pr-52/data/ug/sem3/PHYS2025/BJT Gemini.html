What is a BJT?

The core building block of analog and digital electronics — a three-terminal, two-junction semiconductor device used as an amplifier or a switch.

TERMINAL Emitter (E)

Heavily doped. Its job is to supply a large number of majority charge carriers into the base. It is always forward biased with respect to the base.

TERMINAL Base (B)

The thin, lightly-doped middle layer sandwiched between emitter and collector. It forms two junctions and passes on most of the carriers it receives from the emitter.

TERMINAL Collector (C)

Moderately doped and physically the largest section. It collects the carriers that cross the base. Always reverse biased with respect to the base.

STRUCTURE Two junctions, one bipolar device

A BJT is made by sandwiching a thin layer of one semiconductor type between two layers of the opposite type — giving npn or pnp construction. The term bipolar reflects that both electrons and holes participate in conduction.

Since a single pn junction can be forward or reverse biased, a two-junction device has four possible bias combinations — but only one is useful for amplification: emitter-base forward biased, collector-base reverse biased. This is the active region, covered in section 04.

KEY LAW The current identity

Regardless of configuration, the three terminal currents always obey:

IE = IB + IC

Less than 5% of the emitter current typically recombines in the base to form IB; the remaining 95%+ crosses to the collector as IC. This tiny base current controlling a much larger collector current is exactly what makes amplification possible.

GAIN FACTORS α, β and γ at a glance

α (common base) = ΔIC / ΔIE
Always < 1, typically 0.90 – 0.99.

β (common emitter) = ΔIC / ΔIB
Ranges 20 – 500. β = α / (1 − α).

γ (common collector) = ΔIE / ΔIB
γ = 1 / (1 − α) = β + 1.

NPN vs PNP construction

Same three-terminal idea, opposite doping order and opposite bias polarity. Toggle to compare structure, symbol, and current direction.

STRUCTURE N–P–N

Two n-type layers (emitter, collector) sandwich a thin p-type base. The emitter injects free electrons into the base; the base-emitter junction is forward biased and the base-collector junction is reverse biased.

  • Emitter arrow points outward, away from the base.
  • Majority carriers in circulation: electrons (injected by emitter).
  • Conventional emitter current direction: base → emitter.
  • Requires VCC and VBB with the positive terminal toward collector/base.
Arrow rule: points toward the n-type ~90–95% of BJTs in circuits today are NPN

  • Cut-off: The point where the load line intersects the IB = 0 curve is known as cut off[cite: 6]. At this point, IB = 0 and only a small collector leakage current (ICEO) exists[cite: 6]. The base-emitter junction no longer remains forward biased and normal transistor action is lost[cite: 6]. Both the emitter diode and collector diode are OFF, meaning the collector-emitter pathway is open[cite: 6]. The collector-emitter voltage is nearly equal to VCC (i.e., VCE (cut off) = VCC)[cite: 6].
  • Active: The region between cut off and saturation is known as the active region[cite: 6]. The collector-base junction remains reverse biased while the base-emitter junction remains forward biased[cite: 6]. The emitter diode is ON and the collector diode is OFF[cite: 6]. The collector current is β times the base current (IC = β IB)[cite: 6]. The transistor functions normally here and acts as a linear amplifier[cite: 6].
  • Saturation: The point where the load line intersects the IB = IB(sat) curve is called saturation[cite: 6]. The base current is maximum and so is the collector current[cite: 6]. The collector-base junction no longer remains reverse biased and normal transistor action is lost[cite: 6]. Both the emitter diode and collector diode are ON[cite: 6]. The collector and emitter are, in effect, shorted together, behaving as though a switch has been closed between them[cite: 6]. If base current is greater than IB(sat), the collector current cannot increase[cite: 6].
