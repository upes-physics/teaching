01 · Setting up the coupled system

From two pendulums to two normal modes

Two identical pendulums of length l and mass m hang side by side, each free to swing in its own vertical plane. A light spring of constant k connects the two bobs, at its natural length when both pendulums hang straight down. For small angles θ₁, θ₂, each bob's horizontal displacement is x ≈ lθ.

1
Energy of the system.

Kinetic energy is just the sum of each pendulum's own motion. Potential energy has two parts: gravity acting on each pendulum separately, and the spring, which only cares about the difference θ₁−θ₂.

Kinetic & potential energy T = ½ml²θ̇₁² + ½ml²θ̇₂²
V = ½mgl θ₁² + ½mgl θ₂² + ½k l²(θ₁−θ₂)²
2
Equations of motion. Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation d/dt(∂L/∂θ̇ᵢ) − ∂L/∂θᵢ = 0 to L = T − V for each pendulum gives two coupled equations — each one depends on both θ₁ and θ₂.
Coupled equations of motion θ̈₁ = −(g/l)θ₁ − (k/m)(θ₁−θ₂)
θ̈₂ = −(g/l)θ₂ − (k/m)(θ₂−θ₁)
3
Decoupling with normal coordinates. Neither equation is a standalone oscillator — each depends on the other. But adding and subtracting them cancels the coupling term in one combination and doubles it in the other.
Normal coordinates: q_s = θ₁+θ₂ (symmetric), q_a = θ₁−θ₂ (antisymmetric) q̈_s = −(g/l) q_s   →   ω_s = √(g/l)
q̈_a = −(g/l + 2k/m) q_a   →   ω_a = √(g/l + 2k/m)
4
Two pure normal modes.

Each normal coordinate now obeys its own independent simple harmonic motion — that's what makes it a "normal mode": the whole system oscillating at one single frequency.

Symmetric mode (in-phase)

θ₁ = θ₂ at every instant. The spring never stretches, so it contributes nothing — the system swings exactly like two independent simple pendulums, at ω_s = √(g/l).

Antisymmetric mode (out-of-phase)

θ₁ = −θ₂ at every instant. Now the spring stretches and compresses on every swing, adding extra restoring force — so ω_a is always higher than ω_s, and grows with k.

5
Beats. Release pendulum 1 from angle A with pendulum 2 at rest — a mix of both normal modes in equal parts. Converting back from q_s, q_a to θ₁, θ₂ produces an amplitude-modulated wave: a fast oscillation at the average frequency, wrapped in a slow "envelope" that swaps the motion between the two pendulums.
Beat solution, with θ₁(0)=A, θ₂(0)=0 θ₁(t) = A cos(Δω·t/2) cos(ω̄·t)
θ₂(t) = A sin(Δω·t/2) sin(ω̄·t)

where  Δω = ω_a−ω_s,  ω̄ = (ω_a+ω_s)/2,  beat period T_beat = 2π/Δω

04 · Effect of coupling strength

Sweep the coupling constant k

The symmetric mode never cares about the spring, so ω_s stays flat. The antisymmetric mode always feels it, so ω_a climbs as k increases — the wider that gap, the faster the beats.

Normal-mode frequency vs. coupling strength

ω_s = √(g/l) (flat, amber) and ω_a = √(g/l + 2k/m) (rising, cyan). The marker tracks the slider above; drag the main simulator's coupling slider too — they're linked.
