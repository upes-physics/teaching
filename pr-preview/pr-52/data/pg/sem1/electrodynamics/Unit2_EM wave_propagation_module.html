Layer 1 — Conceptual Understanding

Propagation Parameters of a Plane EM Wave

A time-harmonic plane wave travelling through any linear, homogeneous, isotropic medium — vacuum, a dielectric, or a conductor — is completely characterised by a handful of complex numbers: the propagation constant , its real and imaginary parts the attenuation constant and phase constant , and the intrinsic impedance that ties the electric field E to the magnetic field H. Every medium — free space, a lossless dielectric, a lossy dielectric, or a good conductor — is just a different limiting case of the same four numbers.

Important Observations

  • In a good conductor α ≈ β, so the wave is heavily damped within about one wavelength — this is the origin of the skin effect.
  • The 45° impedance phase angle of a good conductor means H always lags E by one-eighth of a cycle, regardless of frequency or material, as long as σ/ωε ≫ 1.
  • Free space and lossless dielectrics are the α → 0 limit of the same general formula — nothing physically new needs to be introduced for either case.

Summary

γ = α + jβ, α, β, and η are not four independent ideas but one complex-analysis calculation, and , evaluated at whatever σ, ε, μ describe the medium in question. The engineering content of "which medium is this" is entirely contained in where that medium sits between the lossless and good-conductor limits.

Case 1 · Dielectric — Signal Loss in a PCB / Cable Insulator

Substrate material

Physical Principle: A high-speed digital or RF signal travelling down a microstrip trace or coaxial cable propagates through a dielectric substrate (FR-4, Teflon, polyimide) with a small but non-zero loss tangent . Even though the dielectric is not a conductor, σ ≠ 0 gives a small but frequency-dependent α, attenuating the signal.

Mathematical Connection: For a low-loss dielectric, — attenuation grows almost linearly with both conductivity and frequency, which is exactly why dielectric loss becomes the dominant loss mechanism in PCBs and cables as clock speeds climb into the GHz range.

Engineering Relevance: This is the reason high-speed PCB designers specify low-loss-tangent laminates (Rogers, Teflon-based substrates) instead of ordinary FR-4 above a few GHz, and why long fibre/cable runs need periodic signal regeneration — the same α that this module computes is, in the datasheet, printed as "dielectric loss (dB/m) vs frequency."

Key Takeaway: Dielectric loss is a slow, frequency-proportional attenuation — architecturally different from the abrupt skin-effect loss of a conductor, but it comes from exactly the same α = Re(γ) formula with a small σ.

Case 2 · Conductor — Skin Effect in Power & RF Conductors

Conductor material

Physical Principle: Inside a good conductor (copper, aluminium, silver) α and β are both large and nearly equal, , so current crowds into a thin surface layer of thickness — the classic skin effect. At 60 Hz copper's skin depth is about 8.5 mm; at 1 GHz it shrinks to about 2 µm.

Mathematical Connection: comes directly from the good-conductor limit of the same α that governs the dielectric case above — it is the identical formula, just evaluated where σ ≫ ωε instead of σ ≪ ωε.

Engineering Relevance: Skin depth sets the effective AC resistance of power cables (driving the use of stranded/Litz wire at high frequency), determines the wall thickness needed for RF shielding enclosures and waveguide walls, and explains why RF connectors and PCB traces are gold- or silver-plated only a few skin depths deep rather than solid.

Key Takeaway: The same α that is a gentle nuisance in a dielectric becomes, in a good conductor, large enough to confine the entire field to a surface layer — the practical dividing line between "lossy dielectric" and "conductor" is simply where σ/ωε crosses 1.
