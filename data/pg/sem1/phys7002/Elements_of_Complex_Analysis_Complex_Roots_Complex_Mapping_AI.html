1. Complex Number and Argand Plane

A complex number is written as

z = x + iy,   i² = −1.

Here x is the real part and y is the imaginary part.

|z| = √(x² + y²),   arg(z) = atan2(y,x).

In polar form,

z = r(cos θ + i sin θ) = r eiθ.

Geometrical interpretation

  • Re(z) gives the horizontal coordinate.
  • Im(z) gives the vertical coordinate.
  • |z| is the distance from the origin.
  • arg(z) is the directed angle from the positive real axis.
Key idea: multiplication by reiθ scales a complex number by r and rotates it by θ.

3. Complex Differentiability and Analyticity

For f(z), the complex derivative at z₀ is

f′(z₀) = limΔz→0 [f(z₀+Δz)−f(z₀)]/Δz.

The limit must be independent of the direction through which Δz approaches zero.

If f(z)=u(x,y)+iv(x,y), the Cauchy–Riemann equations are

ux = vy,   uy = −vx.

When suitable continuity conditions hold, these equations provide a practical test for analyticity.

Important: Satisfying the Cauchy–Riemann equations at one point is not by itself the same as proving that a function is analytic throughout a region. Always specify the domain and regularity assumptions.

Mapping Concepts

Conformal behaviour

Where f′(z) exists and is non-zero, an analytic function preserves angles locally.

Critical points

Points where f′(z)=0 can change local scale behaviour and may alter the visual structure of a mapping.

Singularities

Functions such as 1/z have points where the mapping is undefined. These must be excluded from the domain.

AI Verification Workflow

  1. Define: State the function, equation, domain and required result.
  2. Prompt: Ask the AI for a derivation, explanation or prediction.
  3. Inspect: Check symbols, assumptions, branches and boundary conditions.
  4. Calculate: Reproduce the key steps independently.
  5. Visualize: Use the interactive simulator to test the interpretation.
  6. Challenge: Ask the AI for a counterexample or edge case.
  7. Conclude: Accept the result only after mathematical verification.
Important: AI can produce plausible but incorrect mathematics. In complex analysis, branch cuts, singularities, argument conventions and domain restrictions are particularly important.

Instructor & Student Support

Instructor & Student Notes

Instructor Notes

  1. Begin with the Argand-plane interpretation before introducing formal notation.
  2. Use multiplication in polar form to demonstrate rotation and scaling.
  3. Emphasize that complex differentiability is a stronger condition than ordinary real-variable differentiability.
  4. Use the Cauchy–Riemann equations together with domain and regularity conditions.
  5. For root finding, ask students to predict symmetry before running the numerical solver.
  6. Use mapping stages to connect algebraic rules with geometric changes.
  7. Discuss singularities and branch choices explicitly for logarithm and reciprocal mappings.
  8. When AI is used, require independent verification of all equations and numerical results.

Student Notes

Remember

  • z=x+iy; |z|=√(x²+y²).
  • z=reiθ.
  • Cauchy–Riemann: ux=vy, uy=−vx.
  • Polynomial roots are counted with multiplicity.
  • Mapping means each admissible z is assigned a w=f(z).
  • Always check domain, singularities and branch conventions.

Common Mistakes

  • Using an unrestricted argument instead of a specified principal branch.
  • Forgetting that division by z requires z≠0.
  • Assuming Cauchy–Riemann equations alone prove global analyticity without regularity information.
  • Accepting an AI-generated root without checking the residual.
  • Confusing a root of P(z)=0 with a fixed point of a mapping.

Technical Note

The root solver is an educational numerical implementation of the Durand–Kerner method. Numerical roots should be interpreted using the displayed residual |P(z)| and not solely by their visual position.

The mapping simulator samples finite grids. It therefore provides visualization rather than a formal proof of mapping properties.
